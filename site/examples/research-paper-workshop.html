Everyone else just has AI write theirs

Maybe, and this skill will still not draft a word of yours. Its first rule bans writing paragraphs, topic sentences, thesis statements, and even "example phrasings" of your ideas. What you get instead is feedback at any depth you want: it will say precisely what a paragraph fails to do, ask the question that exposes the gap in an argument, and point at your strongest sentence and make you explain why it works. The paper that comes out survives an oral defense, because you built every part of it and the skill made you defend the thesis out loud before you submitted.

What you end up with

Your draft, carrying the workshop's annotations. Here is an excerpt from a second draft, with the coach's margin notes beside it. Notice that none of the student's prose has been rewritten.

Example
RAILROAD PAPER — DRAFT 2, EXCERPT
Paragraph 3 of 7  ·  student's words throughout  ·  coach comments in margin
STUDENT DRAFT
The railroad was supposed to connect everyone, but the connection had a map, and the map had a price.
When the Great Basin Line fixed its route through Casper Junction in 1869, the county seat at Milldale lost its freight traffic within two years and its bank within five. 1
One Milldale editor called the route announcement "a death sentence read aloud in a rail office." 2
This shows that the railroad had a very large impact on the towns of the West. 3
CITATIONS, THIS PARAGRAPH
Milldale Courier editorial, 1869 — confirmed opened, cited  ·  freight figures — no citation yet 4
  1. This is doing real work. A specific town, a date, a mechanism. Coach's question in the margin: where does the two-year figure come from? Evidence shown beats evidence asserted, and right now this is asserted.
  2. Good find, but the quote is decorating. The flourish is standing in for analysis. What did the route decision actually change: jobs, land prices, the county seat itself? The quote earns its place once the paragraph answers that.
  3. A sentence with no job. "Had a very large impact" restates the prompt. The coach flags it; the student decides whether to cut it or make it state the next paragraph's claim. The fix is always theirs to choose.
  4. Citation hygiene runs every pass. Every borrowed idea needs a citation the student has actually opened. The workshop walks the list entry by entry and has them confirm each one is real and was read.

Questions people actually ask

Will it write my essay?

No, and it will not write your thesis statement, your topic sentences, or a "sample paragraph to work from" either. It refuses all of it, plainly, then offers the workshop instead: it tells you what a paragraph fails to do, asks the question that exposes the gap, and annotates every draft you bring. Feedback on your words, at any depth, is always in bounds. The typing is yours.

Can it at least reword my clunky sentences?

No rewrites, even "to show you how". It will name what is wrong with a sentence as precisely as you want, and it will point at your strongest sentence and ask why it works so you can do it again. The retype is yours, because that is where the writing gets learned.

Where do the sources come from?

From searches you run together: Google Scholar, JSTOR through your school, your library catalog. If a source only exists in the agent's memory, the skill's rule is to find it live or drop it. Nothing gets cited that neither of you has opened.

Is using this cheating?

What it does matches what a writing-center tutor does: questions, margin notes, and source-checking, with every sentence written by you. Schools set their own AI policies, so check yours; the skill file is short enough to show a teacher exactly what help you got.

Is this free?

The skill is free, no account, no catch. The agent you paste it into may or may not be: Gemini has the widest free tier, Copilot's free tier is enough to try it, ChatGPT and Claude free plans can also run it in a chat.
