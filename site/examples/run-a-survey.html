Five to eight questions, each one serving the decision. Plus a two-sentence consent line up top: what it's for, who sees results, that it's anonymous and optional. Then we pilot it on two people and watch where they hesitate.

What you end up with

Not just responses in a sheet: a one-page finding, delivered to whoever owns the decision. Here is the whole shape of one.

Example
PRACTICE DAY SURVEY  ·  FINDING
for: Coach Ramos  ·  from: the scheduling committee
WHO WAS ASKED 1
All 20 team members, via the team chat, one reminder after four days. 14 answered (70%).
WHAT THE NUMBERS SHOW 2
Preferred day    Sat 9   Sun 3   keep Wed 2
Would attend more often if moved:
                 yes 8   same 4  not sure 2
Open answers: two people mentioned Saturday job shifts. Quoted anonymously: "Sat morning yes, Sat afternoon impossible."
WHAT THIS CANNOT SHOW 3
Six members did not answer, and non-responders may differ from responders. This is a preference count from 14 people; it does not predict actual turnout.
SUGGESTION
Trial Saturday mornings for three weeks and count attendance, then compare with the Wednesday average. 4
  1. Response rate is part of the result. 12 answers from 20 teammates means something different from 12 from a 400-person server, so the finding names who was reachable and who replied.
  2. Distributions before averages. A split crowd and a lukewarm crowd can share a mean, so every counted question is reported as its spread (COUNTIF per option).
  3. Claims stay inside the data. "9 of 14 who answered preferred Saturdays" survives scrutiny; "the team wants Saturdays" does not. The limits section is written with the same care as the results.
  4. Delivered, with a next step. The summary goes to the person the decision belongs to, and the suggestion is checkable against future data.

Questions people actually ask

How many responses do I need?

Enough from the people the decision is about, honestly counted. The skill has you survey people you can actually reach and report n and response rate as part of the finding, so 14 of 20 teammates is a solid basis for a team decision.

Should it be anonymous?

By default, yes, and the consent line says so. You also collect only the personal details the decision needs: if age or name would not change the outcome, the question gets cut.

How do I stop my questions from leading?

Draft, then attack each draft: one thing per question, neutral framing, balanced scales with a genuine middle and a "not sure". Then pilot on two people and reword anything they hesitate over.

What tool does it use?

Google Forms to collect and a spreadsheet to count. Nothing paid, nothing installed.

What if barely anyone responds?

You send once, remind once, and then the low response rate becomes part of the finding rather than a reason to quietly resend until the numbers look better. Two of twenty answering tells you something real about interest.
