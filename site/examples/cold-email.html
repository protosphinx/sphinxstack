Scripted example of a real session.

Why would this person ever reply to me?

They often won't, and the skill is honest about that: silence is a normal outcome and no verdict on you. What earns the replies that do come is exactly what you have: you're young, you built a specific thing, and you did your homework on the person. A short email with a real reason for choosing them and an ask they can answer in five minutes gets read. The skill's job is making sure that's the email you send, and that you actually send it instead of polishing it forever.

What you end up with

One sent email, under 120 words, that you'd be comfortable reading aloud to the person's face. Here is the shape of one that works.

Example
SUBJECT
Question about pinlib's marker engine from a student who built on it 1
BODY
Hi Ana,
Your changelog post said you rewrote pinlib's marker engine because live-updating pins kept tearing. That's exactly the problem I hit. 2
I'm 17 and I built a live bus map for my city with pinlib: busmap.example.dev. To keep markers smooth I re-create the whole layer every tick, which feels wrong. 3
Is there an API I missed for updating markers in place? A one-line pointer would save me a week. 4
Thanks either way,
Maya Torres
  1. The subject states the content. Who is asking and about what. Nothing cute, nothing vague, no "quick question".
  2. Line one could only be about her. It references a real thing she wrote, in plain words. If a first line could be pasted into an email to anyone, it fails.
  3. Who you are, as facts with a link. Age, thing built, URL. No adjectives, no resume attached; links only on a first email.
  4. The ask is small and unmissable. Answerable from her phone in a minute. "Can I pick your brain sometime" would die here.

Anatomy of a subject line

The skill makes you write the subject last, when you know what the email actually says:

Question about pinlib's marker engine from a student who built on it
  • A The topic, named precisely enough that she knows the email is on-subject for her.
  • B Who's asking, honestly. Being a student is context, and it sets the right expectations.
  • C The homework signal: you didn't just find her name, you used her work.

Questions people actually ask

Is this free?

The skill is free, no account, no catch. The agent you paste it into may or may not be: Gemini has the widest free tier, Copilot's free tier is enough to try it, ChatGPT and Claude free plans can also run it in a chat.

Will my email sound like AI wrote it?

This is the one kind of email where that kills you instantly, and the skill treats it that way. Template phrases like "I hope this email finds you well" and "I'm a huge fan of your work" get rewritten as what you actually mean or cut. You draft every line, the honesty check is "would you say it to their face", and anything that fails gets rewritten by you.

How does it find the person's email address?

The honest ways: their personal site, GitHub profile, or a contact page. The skill rules out guess-and-spray and paid lookup tools. If no address is findable, that's usually a signal the person doesn't want cold email, and you pick a different door.

What if they never reply?

Most won't, and the skill says so up front. After a week you send one two-line follow-up with no guilt-tripping, then let it go. A reply, even a no, is a win you answer fast and politely. The email you wrote becomes your structural template for the next one.

Is this for job hunting too?

This skill covers emailing any stranger you want something from: feedback, advice, a question about their work. For the job and internship variant, cold-outreach-for-work handles the extra rules that hiring situations add.
