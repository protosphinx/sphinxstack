What you end up with

Three written artifacts: the offer in one sentence, the ask you actually send, and a scope agreement the client has replied yes to. Here they are for one real-shaped gig.

Example
FIRST GIG: ESSAY EDITING
THE OFFER, ONE SENTENCE 1
I edit college application essays for seniors. One essay takes me about two days, and it costs $25.
THE ASK, SENT BY ME 2
Hi Mrs. Okafor! I've started editing college essays. I did Dani's Common App essay last month and she said it helped a lot; happy to show you. It's $25 per essay with a two-day turnaround. Do you need this for Sam, or know someone who does?
SCOPE, AGREED IN WRITING 3
Deliverable: one edited draft of Sam's 650-word Common App essay with margin comments, plus a 30-minute call to walk through the changes.
Done by: Sunday Nov 9 (padded; I expect to finish Friday). 4
Price: $25, paid on delivery.
Not included: rewriting from scratch, or edits to other essays.
Client reply, Nov 2: "Sounds good, thank you!"
  1. Narrow beats broad. "I edit college essays" sells better than "I help with writing" because the buyer instantly knows if it's for them. One offer only; adding services makes it harder to sell and harder to deliver.
  2. The ask has four parts. What you do, one line of real proof, the price said plainly, and a plain question. No fake urgency and no pretending to be a company.
  3. The reply is the artifact. "Sounds good" under a written scope is a real agreement. Both sides can now check the deal off a list.
  4. The date is padded. First gigs always take longer than expected, so the promised date has slack built in.

Anatomy of the ask

The skill drafts every outreach message to this shape, in your voice:

I've started editing college essays · I did Dani's Common App essay last month, happy to show you · $25 per essay, two-day turnaround · Do you need this, or know someone who does?
  • A What you do, in the narrow one-sentence version.
  • B One line of proof: a link, a photo, or a named piece of past work.
  • C The price, stated plainly. An honest number said without apology.
  • D A plain question that invites a referral even when the answer is no.

Questions people actually ask

I really can't think of anything anyone would pay for.

The bar is lower than you think: it counts if you have done it once for real. The skill walks a list with you (yard work, tutoring a subject you aced, video editing, flyer design, laptop help) until something sticks. If nothing does, it helps you ship one small real thing first so there is proof to point at.

Do I need to be 18, or have a business name?

No business name, no company pretense: you send messages as yourself from your own account. If you are under 18, the skill's rule is that a parent or guardian should know you are doing this, and it says so in the session.

Does the agent contact clients or handle the money?

No. It drafts with you; you send every message from your own account, and you receive payment directly. The agent never touches money or accounts. When the work is delivered, the invoice-and-get-paid skill covers collecting cleanly.

Is this financial or business advice?

No. It is an educational walkthrough of defining an offer, asking real people, and putting scope in writing. Nothing here involves investments or accounts, and questions about handling money go to a parent or guardian and your bank.

What if everyone says no?

Expected: most messages get a no or silence, and one yes is the goal. If the whole list says no, the fix is usually a narrower offer or a lower first price rather than more messages. The skill will not invent testimonials or inflate experience to force a yes.
