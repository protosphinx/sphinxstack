Not sure anyone would pay?

No amount of planning answers that question; a live listing does. The skill keeps the experiment small and honest: one product you can make or find again this week, one channel where your buyers already are, photos of the actual item taken during the session, and a description you could read to the buyer's face. Resold items get described exactly as they are, and services count too. If the first thing does not sell, you have spent an afternoon and learned what a month of logo design would not have taught. Questions people actually ask

I don't make anything. What could I sell?

Things you can resell: thrift finds you know how to spot, collectibles from a hobby you understand, your own outgrown gear. Services count too: lawn care, tutoring a subject you aced, photo editing, dog walking.

How do I price it?

Materials plus your time at a wage you would accept, sanity-checked against three sold listings for similar items on your channel. You set the final number, and the skill will not set it for you.

Am I even allowed to sell at my age?

Maybe with help: marketplaces have age minimums that can mean a parent's account, and food sales have local rules. The skill checks the real answer with you instead of guessing.

What happens when someone actually messages me?

You already agreed on it before the listing went up: how fast you reply, where you meet a local buyer (public place, tell a parent), and how you take payment.

Do I need an LLC or a business name?

No. Ten-product catalogs, logos, and paperwork before a first listing are the trap the skill exists to avoid. The first sale teaches more than any plan, and the log of real listings is the honest version of a business plan anyway.
