​(pastes the skill) parents keep asking what i charge for babysitting and i panic and say "whatever works"
"Whatever works" is a price too, usually the lowest possible one. We'll build a number you can defend, then practice saying it out loud. First, the cost floor: what does one evening of babysitting actually cost you?
nothing? i just show up
Your time is a cost even when it feels free. A defensible floor for it is what you could earn at an available alternative; local minimum wage is a reasonable baseline. If that's $12 an hour where you live and an evening is 4 hours plus $4 bus fare, your floor is about $52 for the evening. Below that, you are paying to work.
$52?? i'd be embarrassed to say that to an adult
Which is why we check the market before you decide anything. Find three real data points on what sitters near you actually get: local listings, what families you know pay, what a sitting service in your area charges. Write down where each number came from. If families near you pay $15 to $18 an hour, your $13-an-hour instinct is already under market. New sellers underprice out of nervousness far more often than they overprice.
fine, say $14 an hour. but when a parent asks me i'll still choke
So we rehearse until you don't. I'll play the parent: "What do you charge?" Your whole line is "It's $14 an hour." No "um", no "is that okay?", no discount before anyone asked for one. When they push with "can you do less?", trade scope for money: "For $40 flat I can do until 9 instead of 11." Then let the silence sit. Ready to run it?

Scared to say the number out loud?

That fear is the actual problem this skill solves, and arithmetic alone doesn't fix it. A price fails if you can't deliver it steadily, so the session ends in role-play: the agent plays the customer, you say the line, it pushes back, you answer with a scope cut instead of a discount, and you run it three or four times until the number comes out flat and calm. What you end up with

A one-page price sheet you keep and reuse: the floor with its math, the market evidence with sources, the chosen price, and the pushback answer, all written down.

Example
PRICE SHEET: BABYSITTING
Written Nov 2026  ·  revisit after the first ten customers
THE OFFER
Evening babysitting, ages 3+, my neighborhood, weekends.
THE FLOOR 1
Time: 4 hr × $12/hr (local minimum wage) = $48
Transport: bus both ways = $4
Floor: $52 per evening (~$13/hr). Never below this.
MARKET CHECK 2
Care listing, my zip code: $17/hr asked (source: listing saved Oct 30)
The Núñez family pays their sitter $15/hr (source: asked them directly)
Community board post filled at $16/hr (source: screenshot, Oct 28)
THE PRICE 3
$14 per hour. Below the going adult rate on purpose, above my floor.
IF THEY PUSH BACK 4
"For $40 flat I can do 6 to 9 instead of the full evening." Scope changes; the rate doesn't.
  1. The floor is arithmetic. Materials at real prices, time at a defensible hourly number, plus the overhead people forget (transport, fees, a share of tools). Working below it means paying to work, which is fine exactly once, knowingly, for a portfolio piece.
  2. Three data points, with sources. Sold and actually-paid numbers beat asking prices. The honest adjustment: the going rate assumes experienced adults, so this price sits below it while staying above the floor.
  3. Closer to market than feels comfortable. That is deliberate. If the floor ever exceeds the market, the offer loses money at market rates, and the skill says so plainly instead of hiding it in the price.
  4. The fallback trades scope. A rehearsed answer to "can you do less?" that cuts the work along with the money, instead of caving on the same work.

Questions people actually ask

Isn't it greedy to charge people I know?

A stated, honest price is fairer to both sides than "whatever works", which forces the other person to guess and usually leads to you being underpaid and quietly resentful. The sheet exists so the number is defensible: you can show the floor math and the market evidence to anyone who asks.

What if my floor is higher than what people actually pay?

Then the offer loses money at market rates, and the skill says exactly that instead of papering over it. The fix happens in the costs or the offer itself (cheaper materials, less time per job, a different service), never by hiding the loss inside a price.

Can I skip the research and copy what someone else charges?

One copied number is one data point, and possibly a bad one. The market check wants three, from sold or actually-paid prices rather than asking prices, each with its source written down, then an honest adjustment for the fact that you may be newer than the people setting the going rate.

When do I raise the price?

After the price meets its first ten real customers, the sheet gets revisited. Selling out instantly means too low; universal balking means too high. Either way you update the sheet rather than abandoning it.

Is this financial advice?

No, and the skill says so in the session: it is not financial advice or business consulting. It is arithmetic plus a market check plus practice saying a number. Nothing here touches accounts, investments, or anyone's money.
