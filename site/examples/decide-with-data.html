Can't I just nudge the weights until my favorite wins?

You can, and the skill watches for it. Nudging weights after seeing totals is called score-fitting, and when it happens the agent names it kindly, because it is a legitimate result: the sheet just told you which option you actually want. That gets written down as the finding instead of being laundered through fake weights. The protections are structural: weights are set before totals are visible, every formula is inspectable, and a sensitivity check shows whether the winner survives moving the biggest weight up and down. What you end up with

A comparison sheet you can defend cell by cell, and a half-page recommendation in your own words. Here is the laptop version.

Example
LAPTOP DECISION
options as rows, criteria as columns, sources noted per number
SCORED COMPARISON 1
criterion   wt   A (Zephyr 14)     B (Swift X)
price       35   $1,049 -> 8.1     $1,299 -> 6.6
battery     25   9.5 h  -> 7.0     11.2 h -> 9.0
weight      15   1.6 kg -> 8.0     1.4 kg -> 9.3
my games    25   58 fps -> 9.0     41 fps -> 6.5
WEIGHTED TOTAL   7.7               7.2
sources: retailer prices 5/12, published battery test, benchmark video for both chips 2
SENSITIVITY CHECK 3
Raising "my games" from 25 to 35 flips the winner to B (7.6 vs 7.5). The options are genuinely close on my stated weights.
RECOMMENDATION  ·  HALF PAGE 4
"A wins by 0.5 on the weights I set before seeing totals, but the ranking flips on a small nudge to gaming, so the numbers call this close. What the sheet cannot settle: B's keyboard felt better in the store, and that matters daily. I'm taking a week to try A's keyboard in person; if it's fine, A."
  1. Normalized so units stop mattering. Each column maps to a 0 to 10 score with the direction flipped where lower is better, in formulas you can inspect.
  2. Every number has a source or a label. Measured facts cite where they came from; subjective calls like "I like the campus" get scored 1 to 5 and marked as judgment. Both belong in the sheet, labeled as what they are.
  3. The stress test is the honest part. A winner that survives weight nudges means a robust decision; a winner that flips means the options are close and the sheet has hit its limit. Either answer is progress.
  4. The write-up names what never fit in a column. Gut feel about people, family factors, fear of missing out. Saying what the sheet excludes is what makes the rest of it trustworthy.

Questions people actually ask

Will the sheet tell me what to choose?

No, and the skill says so in its first breath. It organizes the options, exposes hidden assumptions, and shows how robust the ranking is. The final call stays yours, including everything that never fit in a column.

What if the totals come out nearly tied?

Then the finding is "either is defensible; decide on the unquantified part", and that is a successful analysis. Knowing the options are genuinely close ends the agonizing over which spec sheet to reread.

What counts as a criterion?

Whatever you would regret ignoring in a year, in your words. Five to eight of them, each with a direction and a unit, and overlapping ones get merged because two criteria measuring the same thing act as a hidden double weight.

Some things I can't put a number on.

They still go in. Genuinely subjective criteria get a 1 to 5 score labeled as your judgment, and whatever resists even that goes in the write-up as a named factor the numbers could not settle.

Does this work for big decisions like college?

Yes, with real tuition and aid figures rather than sticker prices, and "do nothing" or "gap year" in the sheet as a real option with its own row. For the major itself, choose-a-major-with-data goes deeper.
