Scripted example of a real session.

Sure every buyer is a scammer?

Most buyers are fine; the trouble is that the scammers arrive first and they all run the same four plays. The skill names them before your listing goes live: the overpayment refund, the fake payment confirmation email, the move to pay outside the platform or in gift cards, and the buyer who asks for a verification code texted to you (that one is an account-takeover attempt). Money is real only when you see it inside the platform's own site or your bank, checked by you directly and never through an emailed link. Once you know the plays, the remaining messages are just people asking about the item.

What you end up with

A live listing with honest photos, honest words, and a checked price, plus a handoff plan and a seller who knows the four scams by name.

Scam filter reviewed: overpayment, fake payment email, off-platform pay, verification code. All four = block.
  1. The flaw photo is mandatory. Six to eight shots in daylight on a plain background, including the damage close-up and one proving it powers on. The flaw photo feels like it lowers the price; what it actually lowers is the odds of a dispute.
  2. Everything stated is checkable. Exact model number from the label, condition stated bluntly, what is included and what is missing, and a boring honest reason for selling.
  3. Sold prices set the number. Three of them, written down with where they came from. What you originally paid is not in the math.
  4. Handoff decided in advance. Public place in daylight, someone along, payment on the spot. For shipped items instead: weigh the packed box, get the real carrier cost, and ship only after the platform confirms payment, with tracking, always.

Anatomy of an honest description

The skill drafts every description to this shape, all of it true:

Nintendo Switch V2, model HAC-001(-01) · works; one scratched corner, see photo 4 · includes dock and charger, no box · selling because I moved to PC
  • A Exactly what it is, with the model number checked on the label.
  • B Condition stated bluntly, every flaw named and photographed. "Like new" only if it is.
  • C What is included and what is missing: box, cables, charger.
  • D The reason for selling, when it is boring and honest. Boring reads as trustworthy.

Questions people actually ask

I'm under 18. Can I even do this?

Most platforms require accounts to be 18+, so the listing may legitimately live on a parent's account, with them involved. That is the skill's default assumption for minors, and the parent should also be in the loop on the handoff plan.

How do I meet a stranger safely for pickup?

Public place in daylight; many police stations advertise safe-exchange spots exactly for this. Bring a parent or a friend, and take cash or the platform's own payment on the spot. Never hold an item on a promise and never deliver to a stranger's home.

When is it safe to ship?

Only after the platform itself confirms payment, and always with tracking. Before listing, weigh and measure the packed item and get the real cost from the carrier's or platform's calculator so shipping is in the price math from the start.

A buyer sent a payment confirmation email. Is that real?

Treat it as fake until you verify it yourself. Money is real only inside the platform's own site or your bank, checked by you directly and never through a link in an email or text. Fake payment confirmations are one of the four standard scams the skill drills before the listing goes live.

Does the agent handle the sale or the money?

No, and this is not financial advice either. You create the listing, answer every message, and receive every dollar yourself; the agent drafts, checks the price math, and warns about the scams. Payment and account questions go to the platform's own pages and, for minors, a parent or guardian.
