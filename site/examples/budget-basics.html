Scripted example of a real session.

Income too small to be worth budgeting?

Small and irregular is the normal case this skill is built for: allowance, a part-time job, gig money. The sheet is built on what actually arrived last month, in your own category names, with formulas doing the totals. The first honest look at the gap between money in and money out is the point of the exercise, and it works at any scale. Nobody sees your account but you: you read your own history out loud, and the agent never accesses anything.

What you end up with

A working spreadsheet: last month's real numbers, next month planned in three columns per category, formulas live, and the monthly review already on your calendar.

  1. Fixed obligations first. Anything owed gets a line before anything optional. Then saving if there is a goal (the save-for-a-goal skill sets that number properly), then everything else splits what remains.
  2. Planned from actuals. November's food-with-friends really was $61, so December plans $45, a cut that can survive a Friday. The actuals column starts empty and gets filled weekly from your own account history.
  3. The buffer is the system working. Around 10% for surprises. An overrun that lands in the buffer is not a failure; it is what the buffer is for.
  4. The bottom line is big on purpose. Formulas keep it current with zero arithmetic effort, and the review changes exactly one number per month. Budgets die of ten simultaneous resolutions.

Questions people actually ask

Do you look at my bank account?

No. You pull up your own bank or payment app history and read the transactions out; the agent never accesses the account, and account or bank questions go to your bank and, for minors, a parent or guardian.

My income is different every month. Does this still work?

Yes; irregular is treated as the normal case. You list what really arrived, with actual amounts and dates, instead of a hoped-for average. Planning then works from last month's real numbers, and three months of accumulated history beats any estimate.

What if I blow the plan in week one?

Two things catch it. The "no idea" buffer absorbs month-one surprises by design, and the plan itself is built from actuals rather than aspirations, so it is survivable in the first place. If a number still turns out to be fantasy, the monthly review renames it: one change per month, chosen at the fifteen-minute check-in.

Do I need a budgeting app?

No. A spreadsheet is the whole tool; Google Sheets is free and fine. Formulas do the totals, the sheet gets copied forward each month so history accumulates, and your category names stay in your own words.

Is this financial advice?

No, and the skill says so: it is not a financial advisor, it is help seeing your own numbers. Nothing here involves investing or touching accounts. Where to keep money and anything bank-related goes to your bank and, for minors, a parent or guardian.
