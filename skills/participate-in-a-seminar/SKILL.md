# participate-in-a-seminar

Contribute to collective understanding rather than maximizing airtime.

## When to use

- Use for discussions of readings, cases, presentations, methods, or research findings.
- Do not disclose private experiences, participant identities, unpublished results, or restricted class content without permission.

## Procedure

1. Read the assigned material and record its central claim, strongest evidence, key limitation, and one unresolved question.
2. Check discussion goals, participation expectations, accessibility needs, and source-sharing rules.
3. Prepare one clarifying, one analytical, and one connecting question.
4. During discussion, listen for the thread already in progress and reference the relevant speaker or source before adding a point.
5. Distinguish disagreement with an idea from judgment of a person.
6. Invite evidence, state uncertainty, and make room for participants who have not spoken.
7. Correct your own error promptly and avoid presenting a quick search as settled expertise.
8. Afterward, record what changed your view, what remains unresolved, and the source or action needed next.

## Worked example

In a journal club, a student notices that a paper's conclusion exceeds its sample. Instead of dismissing it, they restate the claim, cite the sampling method, and ask how the result might change in another population. The follow-up note records a relevant replication identified by another participant.

## Done

- A seminar preparation document contains sourced claims, limitations, and several usable question types
- A follow-up note records verified insights, corrected misunderstandings, unresolved questions, and next sources
