# college-list

Build a college list with a student, from their actual
numbers and their actual constraints. The artifact is a comparison
sheet — eight to twelve schools they would genuinely attend, each
labeled reach, match, or safety, with verified admissions data and
honest cost estimates side by side. The list is theirs; your job is
to make every row true.

## Rule zero: real data, real costs

Every number on the sheet comes from a source you opened this
session: the college's admissions page, its Common Data Set, or the
federal College Scorecard. Never quote acceptance rates, test
ranges, or prices from memory — they drift every year and this
decision is too expensive for stale numbers. And cost gets equal
billing with admissions from the first minute: a school they cannot
pay for is not on the list, however pretty the campus.

## Their side of the equation

- The numbers: GPA (weighted and unweighted if they know both),
  test scores or test-optional intent, course rigor, class rank if
  their school ranks.
- Hard constraints: what the family can actually pay per year — ask
  them to find out if they do not know, because everything hangs on
  it — plus distance from home, size, setting, and any must-haves
  (a specific major, a sport, a community).
- Soft preferences, honestly held: what they want out of the four
  years, in their words. Push past brand names to reasons.

## Research each candidate

For every school they name plus ones you surface from the
constraints, look up and record:

- Admission reality: acceptance rate and the middle-50% GPA and
  test range from the school's own site or Common Data Set. Use reach,
  match, and likely only as rough planning labels, never promises. Admission
  is holistic and no school is guaranteed. A likely option requires both a
  strong admission case and a credible way to pay.
- Cost reality: sticker price, then run the school's own net price
  calculator with the family entering its own figures privately — do not ask
  them to paste tax or account data into the chat. That output, not the
  sticker, goes on the sheet. Note merit-aid patterns and whether
  the school meets full demonstrated need.
- Fit facts: does their major exist, program strength, graduation
  rate, distance, size. From official pages and Scorecard data.

## Balance and build the sheet

- Include several options they would happily attend that appear both likely
  and affordable, plus match and reach options if desired. Replace any school
  they would not attend. The labels organize research; they do not predict an
  individual decision.
- Build the sheet as a spreadsheet they keep: one row per school,
  columns for label, acceptance rate, middle-50 range, net price
  estimate, major, distance, deadline type (ED/EA/regular) and
  date, and a notes column in their words.
- Read the finished sheet together bottom-up, safeties first. If
  the safeties feel fine, the whole process loses its terror.

## Done

- 8–12 schools, balanced, every one they would attend
- Every number sourced this session, net price not sticker
- The sheet saved where they and their family can see it

Next step is the apply-to-college-system skill — this sheet is its
input. And if net prices broke the budget, the scholarship-search
skill works the other side of the equation.
